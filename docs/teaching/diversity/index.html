Course Description

Globalization is usually described in aggregate: trade grew, incomes rose, the world became more connected. Aggregates hide the interesting part. The same process that made a country richer on average made particular places poorer, moved particular people across borders, and left particular groups living apart from each other.

This course looks at four of those consequences in turn, and it looks at them with data rather than assertion. We begin with who paid for globalization: the towns that lost factories to Chinese import competition, and the newer question of where AI is landing. We then ask what those losses did to politics, tracing the argument that economic dislocation feeds populism and weakens democratic institutions. The third session follows people who move — why they leave, what they send home, and what economists actually know about the effect of migration on wages. The fourth comes back to Mexico and asks the question the course title implies but the first three sessions never answer: who lives next to whom, and whether people of different backgrounds ever end up close enough to meet.

Running underneath all four is a second course about evidence. You will see the same moves repeatedly: a map that reveals something an average conceals, two economists reaching opposite conclusions from one event, a correlation that three different mechanisms would produce. By Friday you will have built one of these arguments yourself.

No background in economics, statistics, or programming is required. You will learn enough R to make maps and graphs, and nothing beyond that.

Learning Outcomes

Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:

  1. Describe the uneven geography of globalization — where trade shocks, migration, remittances, and AI exposure actually land — and explain why a national average often describes nobody.
  2. Explain the main mechanisms connecting economic change to political backlash, and distinguish economic from cultural accounts of populism.
  3. Explain why people migrate, why development can raise emigration before lowering it, and what the evidence says about migration and wages.
  4. Measure how unevenly two groups are distributed across a city, and say why the answer depends on the unit of analysis chosen.
  5. Read a claim critically — state what a figure asks you to believe, name what else could have produced it, and say what the data cannot show.
  6. Produce a short empirical report in Quarto containing maps and graphs made in R, with an interpretation written in their own words and an honest declaration of how AI was used.

Assessment

The final grade has three components:

  • Team report — 50%
  • Final presentation — 25%
  • Contributions to class — 25%

Team report. In teams of three, you produce one Quarto report containing two to three maps, one simple graph, one more complex graph, an interpretation in your own words, and a table declaring your use of AI. Worked examples and the datasets are linked from the session pages. Due Friday at 13:10, the start of the final session.

Final presentation. Each team presents its report on Friday. Ten minutes, plus questions. Show your maps and say what they mean; do not read from the slide.

Contributions to class. The average of attendance, participation in the in-class exercises, and progress on the report.

Worked Example Reports

Three finished reports built from this course’s data. They are the model for your own — open one before you write anything, and copy its structure.

  • The China Shock — three maps, a simple graph, a complex graph. The cleanest template.
  • Whose jobs? — the same data asking a question the original authors did not.
  • Ask or delegate? — global AI use, and an honest section on who the data actually describes.

What has to be yours is the question, the choice of variables, and the interpretation. The structure you may take.

Reading

Each session lists readings as Required or Recommended. Required readings should be done before the session that assigns them. Given that this is a one-week intensive course, the required list is deliberately short: one item per session. The required articles are in the shared readings folder.

Software

You will need R, RStudio and Quarto installed before Monday, or a free Posit Cloud account if the installation gives you trouble — it runs RStudio in your browser with nothing to install.

Each session page lists the slide decks from the research workshop to work through that evening. Between them they cover everything the report needs and nothing more: loading a data file, making a graph, making a map, and splitting a graph into panels. The workshop’s statistics and regression decks are not part of this course.

Use of AI

You may use AI in this course, and you must declare it. Every report ends with a short table naming what you used it for, what you asked, and how you checked the answer.

Two rules apply throughout. Never submit code or prose you cannot explain — you will be asked. And use AI for the mechanical parts, not the judgement: fixing an error message is a good use, deciding what your research question is or what your map means is not.
